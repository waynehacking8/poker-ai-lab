# Size-aware opponent models outrank frontier self-play

**The training method that best suits an adaptive Chipzen bot is not on the 2024 to 2026 frontier. It is the architecture you already chose, built in a specific order: a Bayesian posterior over a small library of opponent types whose responses depend on bet size, best responses computed offline against each type under a match-win value, and a cheap equilibrium anchor for the safety blend.** The frontier mostly improved equilibrium learning, which matters less against a field of rule bots. The strongest search-free self-play HUNL agents, VRPO (+33 ± 19 mbb/hand against Slumbot) and MMPO (a statistical tie), optimize chips at a fixed 200 bb. They were trained on two to five times our GPU hours and neither has public code. Every learned fast-adaptation method with published poker numbers was tested on Kuhn or Leduc, and none reports hold'em results at a 30-hand horizon. PSRO has no hold'em result and lost to tuned policy gradient in the largest 2026 comparison. LLMs lose to GTO Wizard AI and cannot run on 0.5 vCPU in 200 ms. The evidence that does transfer says two things decide short matches: the prior and the model class. In the one direct test, an informed prior scored 48 millichips per hand where an uninformed one scored -54. Your lag regression (63% under an oracle prior) is exactly what a model with no bet-size dependence does against a threshold bot. My ranking: (1) measure each opponent's best-response ceiling, (2) make the opponent model size-aware, (3) build match-win value tables, (4) decide by posterior-weighted shallow search, (5) blend against an anchor, and only then (6) spend GPU on PPO, narrowed to a best-response oracle and an amortizer trained with prioritized sampling over the 23 frozen bots. No source tests any method in a short, rising-blind, winner-take-all heads-up format. Every transfer below is inference that only your own harness can confirm.

## Thirty hands are enough only for a strong prior over the right model class

The adaptation literature is unanimous about where its evidence stops. **Every learned adaptive policy with numbers was evaluated on Kuhn, Leduc or BigLeduc, or on non-poker games, and none reports win rate against hands observed at a 10 to 30 hand scale in hold'em.** L2E meta-learns a base policy that adapts in 3 gradient steps, tested on Leduc and BigLeduc ([L2E](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.09381)). GSCU pairs a variational posterior over learned opponent embeddings with EXP3, which picks between a greedy conditioned response and a conservative policy. Its regret is provably no worse than always playing conservatively, but its Kuhn opponents switch only every 1,000 hands ([GSCU](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/fu22b/fu22b.pdf)). OMIS uses just the 3 most recent episodes as context with a 3-block, hidden-32 transformer, but it targets perfect-information games and assumes a known transition model ([OMIS](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/710445227fa8c1b6a9ceada902dd4741-Paper-Conference.pdf)). StratFormer reads up to 3,000 tokens (about 375 hands) through a 4-layer, 512-wide transformer and averages +0.106 BB/hand over GTO across 12 Leduc opponents ([StratFormer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.25796)). AlphaExploitem shows that cross-hand history is worth about half its exploitation gain in Leduc. Masking it cut reward from +1.10 to +0.54 BB/hand on in-distribution opponents ([AlphaExploitem](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.09150)). The only 2026 NLHE in-context result, from LLMs, found a "memory-doing gap": models record opponent tendencies and rarely change their play ([Liu, Li, Schultz](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21710)).

The safe-exploitation line quantifies why statistical confirmation cannot carry a short match. **CS-RNR states that a diffuse frequency shift needs on the order of 10^3 observations to separate from equilibrium, and it releases exploitation against only 1 of 12 opponents in an 800-hand episode** (5 at 2,400 hands, 7 at 5,000) ([CS-RNR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28520)). SafeAdapt-Poker recovers within 8.9 hands of an opponent shift, and 11.9 under bait-and-switch, but only because its encoder and risk model were calibrated offline on the same opponent pool ([SafeAdapt-Poker](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20473)). The cleanest result points at the prior. In Ganzfried, Wang and Chiswick's 3-player Kuhn study, a Bayesian best responder with a Nash-centered prior won 48 millichips/hand, ahead of the best Nash agents at 37. Switching from that prior to an uninformed one dropped it to **-54, second to last**. Starting exploitation at hand 0 or at hand 200 gave nearly identical results ([Ganzfried, Wang, Chiswick](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.06027v4)). The one adaptive method with a hold'em competition result has the same offline-heavy shape. Bard and colleagues' implicit modelling scores a precomputed portfolio of responses online, and it would have won the 2011 ACPC heads-up limit exploitation event ([Bard et al. 2013](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~bowling/publications/b2hd-13aamas-implicitmodelling.html)).

The arithmetic explains both sides. For an opponent with a true 20% VPIP, the binomial standard error is 7.3 points after 30 hands (my calculation), so estimating a free-form strategy inside a match is hopeless. Rule bots, however, deviate hard. An action an equilibrium takes 30% of the time, taken five times in a row, gives a likelihood ratio of about 410 (also my calculation). So a posterior over well-separated types converges within a match, while a posterior over fine-grained frequencies does not. If the match feed carries a bot identifier (your per-bot clones suggest the replays do), the prior can be conditioned on identity. That turns a 30-hand problem into a many-match problem. This is the largest single lever the literature implies, and no paper tests it.

Your lag probe is the model-class failure that this picture predicts. **Lag folds only to bets above 0.75 pot. A single fold rate averages that step function into one number, so even the true average tells the bot the wrong thing at every size.** The likely mechanism is that the bot bluffs at cheap sizes lag always calls and sizes value bets above the point where lag folds. A better prior cannot fix a missing dimension. The oracle prior made lag worse (63%) because it made the bot confident about the wrong function, while it helped station_raiser (85%), whose behavior the class can represent. Deterministic thresholds make likelihoods nearly 0 or 1: updates are fast when the class is right and brittle when it is wrong. The fix is structural. First, model fold, call and raise as monotone functions of bet fraction, per street and per context bucket, with a threshold-and-slope prior fitted from the replays. StratFormer's input is the same idea at a coarser grain: 15 fold, call and raise rates across five strategic contexts ([StratFormer](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.25796)). Second, keep a small epsilon in every likelihood so a wrong type cannot lock in. Third, plug the curve into the bet-versus-check calculation. CSRO's best evolved Leduc policy prices a raise as P(fold) times pot plus P(call) times showdown value, using an estimated fold-to-bet rate ([CSRO](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10098v1)). Make P(fold) a function of size, and the search will bluff lag at 0.8 pot and value-bet it at 0.7. The bet grid has to straddle the thresholds your replays show. The two strongest 2026 HUNL learners, VRPO and MMPO, likewise dropped coarse bet abstractions for fine or continuous sizing. Fit hand-strength dependence with censoring in mind, because folds hide cards and bias per-card estimates ([Safe Active De-censoring](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09954)).

## Frontier self-play RL reaches Slumbot parity on two to five times our GPU hours and the wrong objective

The 2026 model-free results are real but aimed elsewhere. **VRPO (Fan and Farina, May 2026) is the strongest published search-free HUNL policy: +33 ± 19 mbb/hand over 1M hands against the 2022 Slumbot, which its authors call "not a decisive win".** It got there after about 63 hours on 4 RTX 5090s, with no blueprint bet sizes and no subgame solving. Its recipe is PPO with a centralized-critic Expected SARSA advantage, a 0.02 clip, a KL-to-uniform coefficient of 0.1, and evaluation through an EMA of weights at temperature 0.1 ([VRPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.19235v2)). MMPO (Kubíček, Lisý, Sandholm, September 2026) ties Slumbot at +13.5 ± 36.7 over 900k hands. It uses a 4x1024 MLP whose bet head is a 5-component Gaussian over pot fractions, trained for 4 days on an H200 ([MMPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.36787v1)). NashPG beats MMD, NFSP, PSRO and R-NaD head to head, but only in its own 100 bb JAX environment, in unstated units, with no external benchmark ([NashPG](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18183v3)). AlphaHoldem's +111.56 ± 16.06 still cannot be reproduced: there is no code, no weights, and the released hand-history repo is gone. It also cost about 580 GPU hours on TITAN Vs ([AlphaHoldem](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/20394/20394-13-24407-1-2-20220628.pdf)). By my arithmetic, 48 GB10 hours is 0.19 of VRPO's GPU hours, 0.5 of MMPO's if it used one H200, and 0.08 of AlphaHoldem's, before any per-GPU speed difference, which nobody has measured. Neither VRPO nor MMPO has public code that I could find.

The method ranking inside that family is settled enough to use. **The ICLR 2026 reevaluation (over 7,000 runs and 345,000 CPU hours) found generic policy gradient strongest, PSRO "largely noncompetitive" and ESCHER "uniformly noncompetitive".** The winning setups used "more entropy regularization than is typically used for PG methods in single-agent problems" ([Rudolph et al.](https://www.mit.edu/~gfarina/2026/iclr26_reevaluating/iclr26_reevaluating.pdf), [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08938v4)). No model-free neural CFR has a full-HUNL result. The newest, VR-Deep PDCFR+, was tested on Flop Hold'em on a 64-core CPU box ([VR-Deep PDCFR+](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08174)). AlphaHoldem's own ablation still argues for variance control in the update: Trinal-Clip reached Elo 1597 against plain PPO's 1257 at equal data ([AlphaHoldem](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/20394/20394-13-24407-1-2-20220628.pdf)).

The deeper mismatch is the objective. **Every HUNL RL paper found optimizes per-hand chips at fixed 100 to 200 bb stacks, and no 2022 to 2026 paper trains heads-up self-play on match win probability with rising blinds.** The nearest work prices states by tournament continuation values. In a 3-player jam/fold tournament, replacing ICM with values computed from the actual tournament model gained $214.33 of prize equity per hand and was preferred in 2,433 of 2,838 matched units ([ICM Out!](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09586)). Miltersen and Sørensen's older heads-up endgame work already defines state value as tournament win probability ([Miltersen and Sørensen](https://researchr.org/publication/MiltersenS07)). Chipzen starts at 100 bb and depth falls every 20 hands, so a chip-EV learner trained at fixed depth spends most of a match off-distribution. SpinGPT shows the failure in miniature: it over-shoved at 200 bb, a depth missing from its training data, and needed a hand-written patch before it could post +13.4 ± 12.9 bb/100 against Slumbot ([SpinGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22387v2)). Equilibrium seekers also aim at the wrong target. Regularized self-play selects among equilibria, and the author who showed this notes that the choice "matters only against fixed, non-equilibrium opponents", which is the whole Chipzen field ([Leal](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.17543)).

The steelman for your PPO track is real, and it defines the track's job. A policy with a fine bet grid, trained against lag, would find the 0.75-pot threshold without being told. RL needs no hand-specified model class, which is exactly where the decision model just failed. **That makes PPO most valuable as a best-response oracle against each card-aware frozen bot and as an auditor of the opponent model: if a PPO best response beats the model-based search against a clone, the model is missing a dimension.** Its second job is amortization: distill the posterior-weighted search into a small net conditioned on a posterior summary, which is where your value net and expert-iteration work fit. A transformer over raw history is not that net. AlphaExploitem's pool-plus-league training doubled per-hand EV over league-only training, but only in Leduc ([AlphaExploitem](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.09150)). ECHO-2 shows the opposite risk: GRPO against one LLM opponent turned profits against three rule bots (+0.571, +0.514, +0.592) into losses (-0.195, -0.599, -0.451) ([ECHO-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02192)). Three concrete changes follow for the track. First, a terminal ±1 match reward, shaped per hand by the change in the match-win value. Second, rollouts started from mid-match states sampled from replays: data-augmented starts cut exploitability under fixed budgets in long-horizon Kuhn and Goofspiel, though they can bias equilibria ([Lanier et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14379)). Third, attention to curriculum and checkpoint selection over capacity, since the 2026 lightweight-agent study found extra capacity "does little" ([Kelidari et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.06854)). On runtime, a 4x1024 MLP is about 3M weights, or 12 MB at fp32. That is far inside 256 MB and plausibly fast in numpy on 0.5 vCPU, but this is my estimate and no paper reports CPU latency for any HUNL policy. StratFormer-scale context at 200 ms is not credible.

## Leagues pay off only as prioritized sampling over the 23 frozen bots

AlphaStar's league exists to find holes that an adaptive opponent would exploit. **Main agents split games 35% self-play, 50% prioritized fictitious self-play (PFSP) and 15% against forgotten opponents. Exploiters are frozen into the league once they beat their targets in over 70% of games or time out; main exploiters then always reset, league exploiters with 25% probability.** PFSP samples opponent B with weight f_hard(x) = (1 - x)^p, where x is P(win against B), which the authors call "a form of smooth approximation of max-min optimisation" ([AlphaStar](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). In the ablation, adding main exploiters and then league exploiters raised test Elo from 1540 to 1693 to 1824 ([AlphaStar](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). Small-compute successors show how this degrades. TStarBot-X's 5-learner copy collapsed into early rushes within days. Strategy-specific exploiters fixed the collapse, and the largest single robustness gain came from a KL anchor to the policy from about 12 hours earlier ([TStarBot-X](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.13729)). ROA-Star's goal-conditioned exploiters lifted the main agent's worst-case win rate from 1% to 44% against a same-compute AlphaStar replica, using four learners ([ROA-Star](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/file/94796017d01c5a171bdac520c199d9ed-Paper-Conference.pdf)).

Chipzen's field does not learn, so exploiters that hunt for holes in your main agent defend against a threat the gate never tests. **The part of the league that transfers is f_hard over the 23 frozen opponents, because "at least 80% against each" is a per-opponent floor, and f_hard is the smooth max-min.** AlphaExploitem sampled its toy bots uniformly ([AlphaExploitem](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.09150)), which optimizes the average and is the wrong choice for a floor. Keep about a fifth of games against your own recent and K-best snapshots so the policy does not collapse onto the 23 bots. Add perturbed variants of the 10 replay clones, in the spirit of AlphaExploitem's 12 held-out "novel combinations or perturbations", so you do not fit a clone's quirks. Keep at most one main exploiter, and only if the live arena contains learning bots.

PSRO has no hold'em evidence. **Fusion-PSRO, Global PSRO and the other 2024 to 2026 variants report poker results only on Leduc, mostly as figures** ([Fusion-PSRO](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.21027v6), [Global PSRO](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.28273v1)). In repeated Leduc against a fixed population, PSRO with IMPALA oracles scored an aggregate of -45.0, against 39.8 for plain CFR+ ([CSRO](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10098v1)). With 23 fixed opponents, the meta-game against them is a single 23-cell row per snapshot. The one PSRO idea worth keeping is Anytime and Self-Play PSRO's no-regret mixture, which means Hedge weights on per-opponent losses: the same pressure as f_hard, with a regret guarantee ([SP-PSRO](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.06541)). Simplex-NeuPL's idea of one network that best-responds to any mixture over a population is the conceptual match for responding to a posterior. I saw it only at summary level, and it has no poker experiments ([Simplex-NeuPL](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/liu22h.html)).

The gate needs the same worst-case discipline. Average return hides the matchup that fails. In repeated rock-paper-scissors, an LLM agent beat the ContRM baseline on population return (193.2 against 164.8) while doing far worse on worst-case exploitability (67.2 against 16.3) ([CSRO](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10098v1)). **To show a one-sided 95% Wilson lower bound of at least 80% against one opponent, you need about 174 matches at an observed 85%, 68 at 88% and 44 at 90%. Holding all 23 at once (Bonferroni) needs 521, 204 and 131 matches per opponent** (my calculation, rechecked in Python). A full gate at an observed 88% is therefore about 4,700 matches per candidate. Select checkpoints by the minimum per-opponent lower bound, never the average, and hold out a few perturbed bots as an unseen check.

## LLMs lose at the table and earn a place only in offline tooling

The runtime question is closed: no LLM fits 0.5 vCPU and 200 ms. The strength question is nearly closed too. **On the GTO Wizard Benchmark, the best zero-shot model, GPT-5.3 XHigh, lost 16 ± 3 bb/100 to GTO Wizard AI. Folding every hand loses 64.6.** The listed failure modes are unbalanced preflop frequencies, little mixing, and no range modeling ([GTO Wizard Benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.23660)). PokerSkill's scaffolding lifted GPT-5.5 XHigh to -5.7 bb/100. More telling, its rule library alone, with no LLM, scored -13.2 bb/100. That beats every zero-shot entry in the benchmark paper's table, ties GPT-5.5 XHigh on a default prompt (also -13.2), and beats Slumbot's -19.4 from GTO Wizard's own match ([PokerSkill](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30094v1)). That is a strong sign that a deterministic context engine (board texture, hand class, stack-to-pot ratio, size-weighted pressure) makes a good feature set for the anchor and the opponent model. The repo carries a CC BY-NC 4.0 badge, so reimplement the idea rather than copying its tables ([PokerSkill repo](https://github.com/lbn187/PokerSkill)). The RL-trained LLMs teach the same lesson as the small nets. SPIRAL found self-play kept improving where training against fixed LLM opponents stalled, but its games stop at Kuhn poker ([SPIRAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.24119v3)), and ECHO-2's single-opponent overfit is the clearest warning in this whole literature for an arena bot.

LLM arenas say something useful about amateur fields. **In Kaggle Game Arena's 900,000 heads-up hands, GPT-5.2 led at +46.56 bb/100 with a 96.42% VPIP, folding only 7.59% of big blinds to a button steal.** Gemini 3 Pro, which folded 43.78% of big blinds to the same steal, finished at -15.17, and the authors write that its passive defense "concedes significant equity preflop" ([Game Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.31473v1)). Those same models lose to GTO Wizard AI. So aggression wins against a weak, over-folding field and fails against a solid one. That is the case for gating aggression on the posterior rather than baking it into the anchor.

Offline, LLMs are a cheap search operator over code. **CSRO's AlphaEvolve-refined Leduc policy took 110.3 from AlwaysCall against CFR+'s 62.1, and it stayed nearly unexploitable against CFR+ itself (-4.4).** But it took only 42.0 from AlwaysFold, where CFR+ took 57.4 ([CSRO](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10098v1)). One exploit-tuned rule leaves money on the table against other types, which is another argument for type-conditioned decisions. AlphaEvolve's CFR variants posted the lowest exploitability on 14 to 15 of 18 small games, with no hold'em ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16928v3)). CodeClash found that top models "lose every round against expert human programmers" across its code arenas ([CodeClash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.00839)). The defensible uses for a 48-hour run are writing perturbed opponents and new archetypes for the training pool and the hold-out set, plus optional policy-code mutations, judged only by your harness.

The ranking orders the work by expected gain on the gate metric (minimum per-opponent win rate) per hour, subject to dependencies. The first five ranks run mostly on the 20 CPU cores, and I would aim to finish them in the first half of the budget. The GPU is free from hour zero for the rank 6 best-response oracles, which depend on nothing above them; the auditor and amortizer roles wait for ranks 2 and 4.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ceiling probe: best response to each of the 23 opponents in the real format, by exact DP for card-blind bots and oracle-model search for the rest | Your maniac DP reaches about 87%, close to the gate; some opponents can sit below 80% even under perfect play, and no method fixes that | Every opponent has an oracle best-response win rate with a Wilson bound, and any opponent under 80% goes to the owner as a gate question |
| 2 | Size-aware opponent model: fold, call and raise curves over bet fraction, per street and context bucket, with a population-mixture prior over types, epsilon likelihoods and censoring-aware fitting | It fixes the observed lag regression, and the prior is the strongest short-match lever in the literature | Against lag, the oracle-prior run recovers to within a few points of its rank 1 ceiling, and on held-out clone matches the learned prior picks the right type within about 10 hands, reported by hand bucket (1 to 10, 11 to 20, 21 to 40) |
| 3 | Match-win value tables V(stack share, blind level, hands into level, button) per type, with heads-up push/fold solved by your own DP at short depth | Win probability is the objective, and no learner in the literature optimizes it | V matches simulated match outcomes from the same states |
| 4 | Posterior-weighted one-street search over a fine bet grid that straddles observed thresholds, scoring each action by the model's response curves and V leaves | It turns ranks 2 and 3 into decisions, it is exact given the model, and it exposes model errors instead of hiding them in weights | p99 decision latency under 200 ms and memory under 256 MB, measured on a 0.5 vCPU x86 container |
| 5 | Safety blend toward an anchor (whichever of the current heuristic and a coarse blueprint has the higher minimum win rate), weighted by posterior concentration, with a per-match deviation budget | It caps the cost of misidentifying a type | No opponent, held-out bots included, falls below the anchor's own rate |
| 6 | PPO as a best-response oracle against card-aware clones and an auditor of rank 2, then as a posterior-conditioned amortizer of rank 4, trained with f_hard over the 23 bots plus perturbations, a terminal match reward and replay-sampled start states | It finds exploits the model class misses and can buy latency headroom | It ships only if it beats rank 4 on the minimum Wilson bound on the same suite; otherwise it is stopped |
| 7 | Offline LLM tooling for perturbed opponents and hold-out archetypes | It widens the unseen check cheaply | Optional |

A few details carry the ranking. The search in rank 4 responds to the posterior mixture, not to its most likely type. Using per-type V leaves ignores the information value of probing bets, which is acceptable when rule-bot types separate within a few hands, as the likelihood arithmetic suggests. On the flop, our equity against the posterior range needs sampled runouts to stay inside 200 ms. That is an estimate to profile first, along with numpy lookup speed on the target. Rank 5 uses the posterior's own concentration rather than GSCU's bandit, because GSCU's horizons were 250 to 1,000 hands per opponent and EXP3 barely leaves uniform play in 40 ([GSCU](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/fu22b/fu22b.pdf)). It borrows SafeAdapt's idea of a cumulative deviation ledger as the cap ([SafeAdapt-Poker](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.20473)). The anchor needs no deep blueprint. Matches end within two or three blind levels, so solve a coarse MCCFR abstraction with the training-free DCFR schedules (α(t) = 1 + 3t/n, β(t) = -1 - 2t/n, γ(t) = 30 - 5t/n), which the paper did not test with sampled MCCFR, so A/B them on a small abstraction first ([HS-DCFR](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.09097v2)). Hand off to push/fold play at short depth. The chip-EV Nash reference has the small blind shoving 74.1% at 5 BB and 40.9% at 20 BB, tightening against loose callers ([PokerStrategy](https://www.pokerstrategy.com/strategy/sit-and-go/1779/)). By my estimate, the 200 ms limit also rules out the river-only subgame solve that a 1-second budget might have allowed.

Here is how your four active tracks map onto the ranks. The decision-model track becomes the main line (ranks 2 through 5). The exact-best-response track moves to the front as the ceiling probe and as the label source for V. The MCCFR track shrinks to an anchor candidate and stops once it beats the heuristic on minimum win rate. The PPO track keeps its value net and expert iteration as the amortizer, swaps uniform or league-wide sampling for f_hard over the frozen bots, and drops the AlphaStar exploiters. Measure engine throughput on the 20 cores before committing league sizes. No source reports it, and it, not GPU math, decides how many PPO opponents fit in 48 hours. Several items carry uncertainty: the 87% maniac figure awaits arena confirmation, VRPO, MMPO, AlphaExploitem, SafeAdapt and CS-RNR are single-lab preprints mostly without code, and the 10 clones are models of real bots, so passing the gate on clones is evidence about the arena, not proof.

## Conclusion

The frontier and Chipzen want different things. Two years of hold'em RL progress made equilibrium learning cheaper and more stable, but against rule bots in 30 to 44 hands the binding constraints are the opponent model's shape and the objective's shape, and no frontier HUNL training paper addresses either. The lag result shows this concretely: a perfect prior over a misspecified class made the bot worse, and no amount of tuning inside that class would have repaired it. The most useful reframing of RL for this project is as an auditor that finds the dimensions the opponent model is missing, not as the agent.

The gate itself is the open research question. A card-blind maniac is about as exploitable as a bot gets, and an exact best response against it reaches only about 87% in this format, because winner-take-all matches this short carry heavy all-in variance. Some of the 23 opponents could have best-response ceilings below 80%. If so, the right response is a conversation with the owner about the gate, not another training run. That is why the cheapest computation in the plan, the ceiling probe, comes first.
