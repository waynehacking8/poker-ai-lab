# Classic HUNL stack still wins; adapt it, don't replace it

**No 2024-2026 method found beats the classic stack (abstracted MCCFR blueprint, safe subgame solving, restricted-response exploitation) on full heads-up no-limit hold'em in a form you can ship under 0.5 vCPU, 256 MB and 1 s per decision.** The newest search papers are either not poker (Obscuro is Fog of War chess) or tested only on small games (the 2026 equilibrium-refinements paper). The most useful newer result is cheap: training-free hyperparameter schedules for DCFR and PCFR+ that speed up offline solving by orders of magnitude in exploitability at fixed iterations. The newest exploitation papers (July to October 2026) add confidence gates, deviation budgets and showdown-censoring fixes, but they are single-lab preprints tested on Leduc or abstracted HUNL, mostly without code. Open-source HUNL bots are weaker than people assume: the best live Slumbot result from a maintained repo is robopoker at about -13 bb/100, and nothing drops into pure Python plus numpy. Your plan is directionally right. The changes I recommend are about where to spend 48 hours: make the match-win-probability utility and push/fold layer the centerpiece, shrink runtime search to a river-only solve (or drop it), add the hyperparameter schedules to training, and spend the saved time on a simulated opponent field built from your 400 replays. Everything below is a hypothesis about your setting where marked; no source measures play against amateur rule-based bots in short winner-take-all matches.

## What is new in 2024-2026, and what is actually usable

The headline search papers do not transfer. Obscuro (Zhang and Sandholm, arXiv June 2025, revised February 2026) is the first superhuman Fog of War chess AI: 85.1% against the prior state of the art and 16-4 against a 2318-rated human ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01242)). It scores leaves with Stockfish depth 11 rather than a learned net, reports no poker experiments, and has no code link ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)). Its one-sided GT-CFR tree growth is a transferable idea, but a HUNL version needs a leaf value function, which is exactly what ReBeL and Student of Games bought with huge compute. ReBeL used 90 DGX-1 machines with 8 V100 each for data generation and a 6x1536 MLP value net, and reached +45 mbb/hand over Slumbot ([ReBeL](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.13544)). Student of Games reports about +7 mbb/hand over Slumbot ([SoG](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.03178)). Meta's code is archived, Liar's Dice only, no poker ([repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/rebel)); I found no public SoG or Obscuro code. The 2026 "equilibrium refinements" subgame-solving paper claims over 50% exploitability reduction but tests Goofspiel, Leduc and Liar's Dice only, naming Texas hold'em as future work ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17131), [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17131v1)). EVPA (ICLR 2025) claims wins over a DeepStack replication and Slumbot using ensembles of value nets plus pruning and online abstraction, but I could not read its numbers ([ICLR](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/8c1b5863a6b0f925617b917bb2f55be0-Abstract-Conference.html)).

On the model-free side, the freshest poker-relevant result is Deep (Predictive) Discounted CFR (AAAI 2026), which beat DREAM and OS-DeepCFR against five rule-based styles, but only in Flop Hold'em, with 10^8 episodes on a 64-core CPU machine ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.08174), [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08174)). Its code has 20 stars and no license ([GitHub](https://github.com/rpSebastian/DeepPDCFR), stats per the notes' 2026-11-21 API check). ESCHER and DREAM have no HUNL evidence. Neural model-free CFR is therefore not a candidate for a 48-hour build.

The change that matters for you is "Faster Game Solving via Hyperparameter Schedules" (Zhang, McAleer, Sandholm, AAAI 2026). It replaces DCFR's fixed discounting (alpha 1.5, beta 0, gamma 2) with schedules over the iteration count n: alpha(t)=1+3t/n, beta(t)=-1-2t/n, gamma(t)=30-5t/n, derived on three small games and generalized to 17 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09097), [HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.09097v2)). It reports orders-of-magnitude gains in minimum exploitability at fixed iterations, tested on Kuhn, Leduc and two Libratus-generated HUNL endgames, on a single laptop. Caveats: gains are per iteration, not per wall-clock second, and only DCFR variants were plotted on the poker endgames. For tabular training, this is a free change. For MCCFR with sampling (your blueprint), the schedule was not tested, so treat it as a candidate to A/B on a small abstraction rather than a guaranteed win (my inference).

Measurement has also changed. The GTO Wizard Benchmark (March 2026) provides a public HUNL API with AIVAT variance reduction, reports GTO Wizard AI beating Slumbot by 19.4 ± 4.1 bb/100, and shows zero-shot frontier LLMs far below baseline ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23660)). Its API client is public (27 stars, no license field) but needs a key by request form and returns only chips won ([client](https://github.com/gtowizard-ai/researcher-api-client)). Slumbot has no AIVAT, so raw Slumbot results carry wide confidence intervals, and sample sizes differ across papers, so mbb numbers are not comparable ([Slumbot API sample](https://slumbot.com/sample_api.py)). For you, neither benchmark matches the target (Slumbot plays 200 BB stacks that reset every hand), so they are sanity checks for the engine, not strength proxies for Chipzen.

AGPL repos (DecisionHoldem, TexasSolver) are fine to read and to use as offline tools, but copying their code into a deployed bot would carry copyleft obligations, so write your own. None of these ships a pure-Python 256 MB artifact. The robopoker README states that "sampling temperature, not search, is the dominant loss", and its -13 to -28 bb/100 against Slumbot at 200 BB is the honest bar for a serious from-scratch effort ([README](https://github.com/krukah/robopoker)). Your stacks (10000 chips at 50/100 is 100 BB) are shallower than Slumbot's, which is easier, but you have 48 hours, not months. Several maintained solvers (postflop-solver) have gone dormant, and GPU solvers like GTOpen matter to you only as offline tools on the GB10. The proprietary ceiling, GTO Wizard AI, is not available.

For hand evaluation, the notes list pokerkit (pure Python, MIT, 528 stars), treys (pure Python, MIT) and the C-extension eval7 and phevaluator ([pokerkit](https://github.com/uoftcprg/pokerkit), [treys](https://github.com/ihendley/treys), [phevaluator](https://github.com/HenryRLee/PokerHandEvaluator)). Nobody measured speeds on your hardware class, and whether the sandbox allows loading .so wheels is unverified. The safe path is a numpy lookup table built offline.

## What fits 0.5 vCPU, 256 MB and 48 hours

Everything neural or search-heavy fails the runtime test as published. ReBeL needs 720 GPUs for data and about 2 s per hand on GPU-class hardware; Obscuro uses 3 threads and seconds per move; Student of Games has no published light variant. AlphaHoldem is the exception on inference speed (about 2.9 ms per decision on one GPU, trained in three days on one PC), but it is a model-free net with no strong open reproduction (AlphaNLHoldem, 110 stars, AGPL, last push 2023, reports no Slumbot number) ([AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/20394), [repo](https://github.com/bupticybee/AlphaNLHoldem)). A distilled leaf net could fit memory (a 12M-parameter MLP is a few MB in int8, my estimate), but I found no published demonstration, and the real runtime limit is subgame solve cost: a river solve over 1,326-wide ranges for a few hundred CFR iterations is borderline on 0.5 vCPU in numpy (my estimate, not measured).

The offline budget is also small. DecisionHoldem spent 3 to 4 days on 48 cores for roughly 200M iterations of its blueprint, so 48 hours on 20 cores supports a coarser abstraction (unverified scaling estimate). Fit the blueprint to the game you actually face: with 10000-chip stacks and blinds climbing every 20 hands, effective stacks collapse to push/fold depth within a few levels, so the postflop tree you must solve well is smaller than a 200 BB game's. Per-stack blueprints stay in the tens of MB if stored as float16 or uint8 tables (inference from the notes' sizing).

The exploitation stack divides cleanly into offline and online parts. The Johanson and Bowling data-biased robust counter-strategy builds a family between best response and Nash, constraining the opponent model per information set according to data confidence ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v5/johanson09a.html)), so rarely observed spots stay near equilibrium. That matches a 400-replay log. Its runtime cost is a table lookup. Ganzfried and Sandholm's safe exploitation needs an LP per iteration over the whole game ([NeurIPS 2022 description](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/b12a1d1014e952e676f5d6931d03241a-Paper-Conference.pdf)), which is feasible only if precomputed offline in the abstraction, with the runtime reduced to counters and a gate. The newer papers add three usable ideas. Confidence-Scheduled Restricted Responses (CS-RNR, July 2026) lets a deviation count only once its confidence interval separates from the equilibrium reference and certifies each candidate by full-tree best response ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28520)). SafeAdapt-Poker (October 2026) bounds total deviation with a cumulative-KL ledger against an anchor policy, reporting +42.7 mbb/hand in abstracted HUNL cross-play ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20473)). Safe Active De-censoring (August 2026) addresses the fact that folds hide private cards, which biases per-card estimates fitted from logs; it has MIT code but one star and a 10^6-hand evidence scale ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09954), [repo](https://github.com/jiaxing-guo/safe-observation-capacity)). Treat all three as unreplicated; their value to you is design guidance, not code. The runtime-search exploitation papers (OX-Search at ICML 2024, Adapting Beyond the Depth Limit at AAMAS 2025) have no found code and need a subgame solve per decision ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/ge24b.html), [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10464)). Transformer meta-agents (StratFormer, AlphaExploitem) are Leduc-scale or unquantified and have no code ([StratFormer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25796), [AlphaExploitem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09150)). LLM hybrids such as ToolPoker need an LLM at runtime and still trail CFR+ ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00528)).

The expected gain against your field is the weakest-evidence part. No source quantifies exploitation margins against heterogeneous rule-based bot fields. The Alberta group's historical note says their exploiters sometimes won single matchups but near-equilibrium play often did better overall ([Alberta news](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~games/poker/news_2010.html), as summarized by the notes, date unverified), and winners in every ACPC year used CFR ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/poker-ai-2016-annual-computer-poker-competition-24246.htm)). My reading: against genuinely weak bots an equilibrium-like bot already wins most matches, so exploitation adds margin mainly where ranking rewards margin, and its risk is spending variance on a wrong model. In a 44-hand median match you get roughly 20 to 40 hands of evidence per opponent, so within-match adaptation is nearly useless; what carries value is the prior fitted offline from your replays plus coarse, fast classification (for example fold-to-raise frequency after a handful of hands).

## Match win probability, not chips, is the lever with the clearest support

Winner-take-all means utility is the probability of winning the match. Chip share maps monotonically to win probability but not linearly. Miltersen and Sorensen compute near-optimal heads-up endgame strategies with game values equal to tournament win probability, and show a player holding 2500 of 8000 chips has a guaranteed win probability strictly above 2500/8000 at 300/600 blinds ([paper](https://www.itu.dk/~trbj/papers/poker.pdf), summarized via [researchr](https://researchr.org/publication/MiltersenS07); the notes did not read the full text). Jam/fold play is near-optimal in the two-player case ([Ganzfried and Sandholm 2008](https://aamas.csc.liv.ac.uk/Proceedings/aamas08/proceedings/pdf/paper/AAMAS08_0313.pdf)). I found no 2024-2026 paper that trains CFR or RL on match win probability, so this is old, well-understood theory with no recent competitor. The natural implementation is dynamic programming over (stack, blind level) states with the win probability at the terminal states, solving short-stack nodes as push/fold Nash, rather than RL (my inference).

The numbers matter. A chip-EV Nash table gives SB shoves of 74% at 5 BB falling to 41% at 20 BB, and BB calls of 69% falling to 22% ([PokerStrategy](https://www.pokerstrategy.com/strategy/sit-and-go/1779/)). That page also gives the exploit direction: against loose callers, stop shoving your weakest Nash hands; against tight callers, add hands. With about 55% big-blind defense in your replays versus a 38.5% Nash call at 10 BB, loose-caller logic applies, so shove narrower than Nash against that population (inference from the notes). Other chart mirrors disagreed with this table and I rely on none of them. I found no permissively licensed HU 5-40 BB chart set, so compute your own: it is a 169x169 matrix game and cheap on the GB10. Your schedule (blinds up every 20 hands, median 44 hands) means most matches end within about two to three levels, so the push/fold layer and the transition into it matter more than perfect deep-stack postflop play. Caveat: the "favorite should avoid variance, underdog should seek it" intuition and any claim that a skill edge erodes as blinds rise are my reasoning, not sourced.

## Ranked changes to the plan

The order below is by expected value per hour of effort, assuming the field is mostly amateur rule-based bots.

First, make the win-probability utility and push/fold solution the core, not a utility swap inside the blueprint. Build the (stack, blind level) dynamic program with your own push/fold Nash at depths of roughly 3 to 25 BB, using the actual blind schedule, and use it wherever effective stack is below the point where open-raise lines vanish. Pair it with the loose-caller adjustment above. Support is the strongest in this report because it rests on a rigorous published line and on the structure of your format.

Second, demote runtime safe subgame solving. At 0.5 vCPU and 1 s, a numpy turn solve is not credible, and a river solve is borderline; in short matches against weak bots the gain is also small (inference). Replace turn solving with blueprint lookup plus a cheap equity-based correction, and keep at most a river-only solve built from the MIT-licensed Python reference in noambrown/poker_solver, with a hard time cap and blueprint fallback. If your first profiling run shows a river solve exceeds about 0.5 s, drop it entirely and spend the effort below.

Third, add the hyperparameter schedules to offline training of the blueprint and of any offline restricted-response solve. It costs a few lines, helps exactly the part of the plan that stays. Validate on a small abstraction against fixed DCFR before the long run, because the sampled-MCCFR case was not tested in the paper.

Fourth, keep the Johanson and Bowling fit but build it from the 400 replays with fold-hidden cards in mind (the Safe Active De-censoring paper's point), and add a deviation budget plus an equilibrium anchor in the style of CS-RNR and SafeAdapt-Poker: every deviation from the blueprint is capped and gated by simple per-opponent counters. Precompute the restricted-response tables offline per archetype, not per opponent. Use Ganzfried and Sandholm's safety guarantee as the cap, not as a runtime LP.

Fifth, build a simulated field before anything else gets tuned. Cluster the replays into archetypes (the notes suggest 75% button raise and 55% big-blind defense as observed anchors), clone each as a fitted policy, and evaluate every candidate as a match-win rate over the 44-hand format, not as mbb/hand. No published source gives numbers for this setting, so your own harness is the only evidence you will get; it also guards against a policy that wins chips but loses matches. Run the same-suite baseline discipline: re-run the plain blueprint on the same cases before claiming any exploit gain.

Sixth, treat the engineering items as risks: a numpy 7-card lookup table instead of an extension that the sandbox may reject, float16/uint8 blueprint storage kept well under 256 MB, and a blueprint fallback on any timeout. Do not add a neural net, an LLM, a transformer meta-agent or an ABD/OX-Search runtime; none fits the limits or has HUNL evidence plus usable code.

## Conclusion

The practical frontier for a weak, short-format arena has barely moved in the places you can ship: the winning recipe is still a coarse equilibrium prior plus a gated exploit. The new knowledge is negative and procedural. No new HUNL search method has open code you can use, the best open Rust stack still trails Slumbot, and the only 2024-2026 gain that clearly transfers is a free schedule for the offline solver. Most of the possible edge sits in the objective (win probability, push/fold depth) and in how faithfully you simulate the opponents, neither of which a 2026 paper will supply.

Several facts here rest on abstracts, snippets or unread PDFs, and are flagged where used: EVPA's numbers, the Johanson and Bowling and Ganzfried and Sandholm 2015 empirical results, the Alberta note's date, Miltersen and Sorensen's full text, and all Python-level speeds on your hardware. chipzen.ai itself was not visited in any of the underlying research. Verify the blind schedule and the actual stack depths there before sizing the abstraction.
